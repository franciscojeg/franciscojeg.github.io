What AIB-C01 actually tests

This exam doesn't expect you to code or configure AWS. It expects you to make sound business decisions about AI: where to use it, how to measure it, how to govern it, and how to get the organization to adopt it.

  1. Start with the problem: which business outcome needs to improve — revenue, cost, time, satisfaction, risk, or innovation?
  2. Validate whether AI is necessary: a simple rule can be more transparent, cheaper, and more appropriate.
  3. Prioritize: expected value, feasibility, available data, risk, sustainability, and strategic alignment.
  4. Design the adoption: governance, owners, training, baseline metrics, and iterative improvement.
Guiding question: "What is the most responsible decision that maximizes business value under the given constraints?" On this exam, an answer with objectives, metrics, risk control, and an adoption plan usually wins over an isolated technical choice.

Hint: "tabular data with a fixed, stable rule" may call for rules-based automation, not AI. "complex patterns that change" is a stronger signal for ML.
Data, agents, and GenAI
  • Structured data: rows/columns, like a sales table. Unstructured: text, image, audio, and documents.
  • Data quality: incomplete, biased, or outdated data produces weak results even with a good model.
  • AI agent: doesn't just respond; it can plan, use tools, and execute steps to reach a goal. That's why it needs limits and oversight.
  • Prompt: the instruction that guides the output. Be clear about role, task, context, format, and limits.
  • Tokens and context: limit how much content the model can process; more context increases cost and can distract it.
RAG vs. fine-tuning

RAG adds relevant documents at the moment of responding; it's useful for internal, changing information. Fine-tuning adjusts the model's behavior using examples; it requires more data, evaluation, and management. From a business standpoint, RAG is usually the first option when the problem is up-to-date knowledge.

Domain 2 · Strategy and Value Creation 28% · heaviest weight

From use case to AI portfolio

A use case doesn't start with "we want to use AI." It starts with a concrete pain point or opportunity. Define the affected population, the current process, the expected outcome, available data, and risks.

CriterionPractical question
ValueDoes it increase revenue, cut cost/time, improve experience, or mitigate risk?
FeasibilityIs there data, a sponsor, capacity, and a process to integrate it into?
AlignmentDoes it support a real strategic priority?
RiskCould it affect rights, reputation, compliance, or security?
ScalabilityCan it be reused or grow from pilot to enterprise capability?
Hint: if two proposals both have value, prioritize the one that combines high impact, good feasibility, ready data, and manageable risk. Don't prioritize on novelty alone.
Measuring value: KPI, baseline, and ROI
  • Baseline: measures the current state before introducing AI. Without a baseline you can't attribute improvement.
  • Tangible KPI: savings, revenue, cycle time, conversion rate, cost per case.
  • Intangible KPI: customer satisfaction, perceived productivity, decision quality, or learning.
  • ROI: compares expected benefits against implementation, operation, change, and risk costs. Also report your assumptions.
  • Adoption: active use, recurrence, acceptance rate, and percentage of assisted tasks; a solution nobody uses generates no value.
Trap: measuring only model accuracy doesn't demonstrate business value. Connect technical quality to a process or business outcome metric.
Build, buy, or partner
OptionWhen it may make sense
BuyStandard need, short timeline, lower operational complexity.
BuildClear strategic differentiation, custom requirements, and the capacity to sustain it.
PartnerLacking internal capabilities or needing to accelerate with specialized expertise.

Compare total cost, time, talent, control, integration, data, compliance, and dependency risk; don't decide on initial price alone.

For high-impact decisions — for example, employment, credit, health, or safety — raise the level of human review, explainability, and control.
Governance and enterprise risk
  • Define a committee or structure with business, legal, risk, data, security, and technology; AI shouldn't be left without an owner.
  • Classify tools: approved, under evaluation, or blocked. This reduces shadow AI: unauthorized use of AI with corporate data.
  • Maintain an inventory of use cases, data, vendors, risks, controls, owners, and reviews.
  • Compliance is continuous: regulatory changes, new data, and updated models can change the risk.
Hint: "employees use public tools with sensitive information" → a clear policy, approved tools, training, and controls; not just a blanket prohibition.

Domain 4 · Readiness, Leadership, and Transformation 24%

Assessing readiness for AI

AI maturity isn't measured by models alone. Assess four dimensions: people (skills and leadership), processes (use cases and governance), data/technology (quality, access, and integration), and culture (trust, experimentation, and change management).

  • Identify gaps before launching at scale.
  • Start with narrow pilots, hypotheses, and metrics; learn before expanding.
  • Build capabilities: AI literacy for everyone and role-specific training.
  • Manage the change: communicate impact, involve users, and redesign processes, not just tools.
Scaling a pilot responsibly
  1. Validate: does the pilot improve its KPI against the baseline?
  2. Decide: scale, iterate, pause, or terminate based on evidence.
  3. Industrialize: standards, governance, support, data, and ownership.
  4. Extend: reuse components/learnings in other areas without blindly copying the original case.
Trap: a successful demo doesn't equal enterprise readiness. Before scaling, validate adoption, data, risk, cost, and operational capacity.
