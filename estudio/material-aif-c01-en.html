0. Before you start: what this exam is like
AIF-C01 is a foundational-level exam. It doesn't ask you to write code, train models by hand, or tune hyperparameters. It asks you to understand what AI, machine learning, and generative AI are, which AWS service solves each type of problem, and how to use them responsibly and securely.
FactValue
Questions65 (50 scored, 15 unscored for calibration, with no way to tell which is which)
Duration90 minutes, so less than a minute and a half per question: a tighter pace than SAA-C03
Score100 to 1000, with 700 as the minimum to pass
CostUSD 100
PrerequisitesNone formally required
DomainContentWeight
Domain 1Fundamentals of AI and ML20%
Domain 2Fundamentals of Generative AI24%
Domain 3Foundation Model Applications28%, the heaviest
Domain 4Guidelines for Responsible AI14%
Domain 5Security, Compliance and Governance for AI Solutions14%
The most important mindset shift: on this exam there's almost never a single option "that works." A chatbot that needs company-specific knowledge can be solved with fine-tuning or with RAG, and both technically work. The option with lower cost and lower maintenance effort is usually RAG, unless the question explicitly asks for the model's underlying behavior to change. The question to always ask yourself isn't "does this work?" but "is this the most appropriate option given what the question asks for: cost, effort, implementation speed, or accuracy?"
How to approach every question, in two rounds: first rule out the options that don't solve the type of problem posed, for example a computer vision service doesn't help with a text task. Then, if several viable options remain, decide based on the specific constraint in the question: cheapest, least maintenance effort, shortest implementation time, or highest accuracy. Pay special attention to whether the scenario describes a business use case, which points to a specialized AI Service or Bedrock, or a need to build and train your own model from the data, which points to SageMaker.

1. Fundamentals of AI and ML 20% of the exam
This is where the base vocabulary for the whole exam gets built. If you find the terms AI, ML, Deep Learning, and Generative AI confusing, start here before moving on to anything else.
The concept hierarchy, explained as Russian nesting dolls:

Picture four circles, one inside the other. The biggest circle is Artificial Intelligence (AI): any system that mimics human capabilities, like recognizing a face or translating a language, no matter how it pulls it off internally.

Inside that circle sits Machine Learning (ML): the part of AI where, instead of programming explicit rules ("if the email contains word X, it's spam"), the system learns patterns from data examples ("I show it thousands of emails already labeled spam or not spam, and the system learns on its own what tells them apart").

Inside ML sits Deep Learning (DL): a way of doing ML using neural networks with many stacked layers, loosely inspired by how neurons in the brain work. It's the technique that lets a system recognize objects in photos or understand natural language with far more accuracy than simpler ML techniques.

And inside Deep Learning sits Generative AI: deep learning models trained on so much data that, instead of just classifying or predicting, they can generate new content (text, images, audio, code) that didn't exist before.

In short: all Generative AI is Deep Learning, all Deep Learning is Machine Learning, and all Machine Learning is Artificial Intelligence, but not the other way around.

Neural networks: the engine behind Deep Learning

A neural network is, at its core, a chain of layers of simple mathematical calculations that, combined, can learn complex patterns. You don't need to do the math, but you do need to recognize the pieces and what each one is for, because the exam names them directly.
ComponentWhat it is, in plain wordsPriority
NeuronThe network's smallest unit of computation; it receives numbers, combines them, and produces an output number.Medium
LayersGroups of neurons organized into levels. The input layer receives the raw data, the hidden layers do the intermediate processing, and the output layer delivers the final result.Medium
Weights and biasesThe "adjustable numbers" the network keeps tweaking during training to improve its predictions. Training a model is, literally, finding the best values for these numbers.Medium
Activation functionA mathematical rule that decides whether a neuron "fires" or not based on its input, which lets the network learn complex relationships instead of only straight lines.Low
Forward pass and BackpropagationForward pass is when data moves through the network to produce a prediction. Backpropagation is the process where the network compares that prediction to the correct answer and adjusts the weights backward to improve next time.Low
Gradient DescentThe method the network uses to decide which direction to adjust each weight in, aiming to reduce the error step by step.Low
Vanishing / Exploding GradientsTechnical problems where the adjustments during training become too small (vanishing, the network stops learning) or too large (exploding, the network becomes unstable).Low

Types of neural network architectures

ArchitectureWhat it's forPriority
FNN (Feedforward)The most basic architecture, where data flows in a single direction, from input to output.Low
RNN (Recurrent)Designed for sequential data, where order matters, such as time series or text word by word.Low
CNN (Convolutional)Specialized for images and video, detects visual patterns like edges, textures, and shapes.Medium
GAN (Generative Adversarial Network)Two networks competing against each other, one generating fake content and the other trying to detect it, both improving over time. Used to generate realistic images.Low
TransformerThe architecture behind modern LLMs (GPT, Claude, Titan). Processes the whole text at once instead of word by word, "paying attention" to which parts of the text are most relevant to each other.High

Key domains within AI

DomainWhat it covers
Computer VisionInterpreting images and video: detecting objects, faces, text in images.
NLP (Natural Language Processing)Understanding and generating human language: sentiment, entities, translation, summarization.
LLM (Large Language Model)A language model trained on huge volumes of text, capable of conversing, reasoning, and generating text across multiple tasks.
SLM (Small Language Model)A smaller, lighter version of a language model, with lower cost and latency, useful when you don't need the full capability of an LLM.

Learning types (Machine Learning Paradigms)

TypeQuestion it answersExample
Supervised learning "I have examples where the correct answer is already known, can I learn to predict it?" Predicting a house's price from historical data where the actual sale price is already known.
Unsupervised learning "I don't have known answers, can the system find structure in the data on its own?" Segmenting customers into groups based on purchasing behavior, without telling it beforehand what the groups are.
Reinforcement learning "Can the system learn by trial and error, receiving a reward or penalty based on its actions?" An agent that learns to play a video game, improving every time it scores points.
Self-supervised learning "Can I generate my own labels from the data without human intervention?" This is the technique behind most of the training of modern LLMs: the model learns to predict the next word of a text using the text itself as the source of the correct answer.

Data types

TypeExample
Structured dataTables with well-defined rows and columns, like a spreadsheet or a relational database.
Semi-structured dataHas some organization but no rigid schema, like JSON or XML files.
Unstructured dataNo predefined organization: free text, images, audio, video.
In any dataset, features are the input variables the model uses to learn (for example, a house's square footage and location), and the label is the answer you want to predict (the house's price). Only supervised learning needs labels.

Model fit: overfitting, underfitting, bias, and variance

ConceptWhat it meansPriority
Overfitting The model "memorizes" the training data, noise included, and performs very well on that data but poorly on new data. It's like a student who memorizes the exact answers to a practice exam but doesn't understand the concept, so they fail the real exam with different questions. High
Underfitting The model is too simple and doesn't even learn the training data well. It's like a student who barely studied: they fail both the practice run and the real exam. High
Bias (model bias) Error that comes from the model being too simple to capture the real relationship in the data, which usually produces underfitting. Medium
Variance Error that comes from the model being too sensitive to small changes in the training data, which usually produces overfitting. Medium

Inference: how an already-trained model gets used

TypeWhen to use it
Real-time inferenceWhen an immediate response is needed, like a chatbot answering a live user.
Batch inferenceWhen a lot of data is processed at once with no need for an immediate response, like generating overnight classification reports for thousands of documents.

Machine Learning project lifecycle

Business problem definition → data collection and cleaning → exploratory data analysis (EDA) → feature engineering → model training → model evaluation → deployment → continuous monitoring. Monitoring is key because models suffer drift: they perform worse over time because real-world data changes and stops resembling the data the model was trained on.

Basic evaluation metrics

MetricWhat it measuresPriority
Accuracy Of all the predictions made, what percentage was correct. Easy to understand but misleading when classes are imbalanced. High
Precision Of everything the model flagged as positive, how much was actually positive. Matters when the cost of a false positive is high. High
Recall Of all the actual positive cases that existed, how many the model caught. Matters when the cost of missing a real positive case is high. High
F1 Score A balance between precision and recall, useful when both matter and you can't pick just one. Medium
The exam doesn't require calculating these metrics by hand, just recognizing which scenario each one matters most in. Classic example: in fraud detection, recall matters more, because letting a real fraud slip through is far more costly than over-investigating a transaction that turns out to be legitimate, even if that generates more false positives.

When AI isn't the right solution

The exam also asks when NOT to use AI. Warning signs: the problem has an exact, deterministic answer that can be solved with simple business logic (you don't need a probabilistic model for that), the cost of building and maintaining a model outweighs the expected benefit, or the domain is highly regulated and sensitive, where a model error has serious legal or safety consequences and full, explainable control over every decision is required.

2. Fundamentals of Generative AI 24% of the exam
This is where the generative AI-specific vocabulary gets built: tokens, embeddings, context window, temperature. These terms reappear throughout Domains 3, 4, and 5, so it pays to master them here.

Core concepts, explained from the ground up

ConceptPlain-language explanationPriority
Foundation Model A large-scale model, trained on huge amounts of general data, that serves as an adaptable base for many different tasks without having to train a new model from scratch for each one. It's like a person with broad general knowledge: they can write, summarize, translate, or answer questions without having specifically studied each of those tasks separately. High
Token The smallest unit of text a language model processes, roughly a word or a word fragment. The cost of using a generative model and the limits on how much text it can handle at once are measured in tokens, not words or characters. High
Chunking Splitting a long document into smaller fragments (chunks) before converting it into embeddings, because a model can't process unlimited amounts of text at once. Chunk size affects the quality of later searches in RAG. Medium
Embedding Converting text, an image, or other data into a list of numbers (a vector) that represents its meaning. Two texts with similar meanings will have similar vectors, even if they use different words. This lets you search "by meaning" instead of by exact word matching. High
Context window The maximum number of tokens a model can factor into a single interaction, counting the input prompt plus the generated response. If the conversation or document exceeds that limit, the model simply can't "see" what fell outside it. High
Temperature A parameter that controls how predictable or creative the model's response is. Low temperature produces more consistent, repeatable responses across different runs, useful for tasks where accuracy matters. High temperature produces more varied, creative responses, useful for creative content generation. High

Limitations you need to recognize

LimitationWhat it means
HallucinationThe model generates information that sounds convincing and confident, but is incorrect or outright made up.
Non-determinismThe same prompt can produce different responses across different runs, even at the same temperature.
BiasThe model can reproduce biases present in the data it was trained on.
ToxicityThe model can generate offensive, harmful, or inappropriate content if it isn't controlled with guardrails.
Intellectual propertyRisk that the generated content resembles protected works used in training too closely, or that using the generated content carries unclear legal implications.

How a hallucination gets detected and mitigated

Beyond RAG, Bedrock offers two distinct, complementary mechanisms for reducing hallucinations. The contextual grounding check in Bedrock Guardrails verifies that a response is grounded in a supplied reference source (grounding) and is relevant to the user's question (relevance); each dimension gets a confidence score from 0 to 1, and a response below the configured threshold gets blocked. It applies to summarization, paraphrasing, and question answering, not to full conversational chatbots. Automated Reasoning checks, on the other hand, translate the model's claims into formal logic and mathematically verify them with SMT solvers against business rules encoded in a policy; unlike the grounding check, the final validation step is deterministic and doesn't depend on another LLM.

Amazon Bedrock

Bedrock is the service that gives you access, through a single API, to foundation models from different providers (Anthropic, Meta, Amazon, and others), without having to manage servers or your own compute infrastructure. Think of it as a store carrying several brands of already-trained models, ready to use right away.
Bedrock componentWhat it doesPriority
Knowledge BasesConnects a foundation model to your own documents to implement RAG, without building the vector store yourself from scratch.High
AgentsLets the model carry out multi-step tasks by invoking external tools, such as functions or APIs, instead of only replying with text.Medium
GuardrailsApplies content and safety policies to the model's inputs and outputs, blocking unwanted topics or sensitive information.High
Prompt ManagementCentralizes prompt creation, versioning, and testing outside the application code: double-curly-brace variables, side-by-side variant comparison, and deploying immutable versions without touching the code.Medium
PartyRockA free environment for experimenting with and building simple generative apps on top of Bedrock, no full AWS account required.Low
Bedrock vs SageMaker, this domain's most-asked distinction: Bedrock is for quickly consuming and adapting already-trained foundation models, without managing infrastructure. SageMaker is for building and training your own machine learning models from the data, including training or fine-tuning foundation models when you need more technical control than Bedrock offers.

Other generative services

ServiceWhat it is
Amazon QAWS's generative assistant, with variants aimed at developers (helps with code) and at business (answers questions about enterprise data).
AWS App StudioTool for building internal business applications using generative AI, with little code.

Cost models in generative AI

ModelWhen it makes sense
Pay per token (on-demand)Ideal when usage is variable or unpredictable; you pay for what you actually consume, no commitment.
Provisioned ThroughputReserves guaranteed capacity for a period, makes sense when usage is constant and high and you need predictable latency.
Custom modelsInvolve an additional training or fine-tuning cost, but can be more efficient long-term for a very specific use case.

3. Foundation Model Applications 28% of the exam, the heaviest
This is the domain with the most questions. Master RAG especially, prompt engineering techniques, and the specialized AI services table, since a large share of the whole exam comes from there.

Criteria for choosing a pretrained model

CriterionWhat it evaluates
CostPrice per token or per hour of use; the biggest model isn't always the right answer if the requirement calls for the most cost-effective option for a simple task.
ModalityWhether the model supports only text, or also images, audio, or other modalities (multimodal).
LatencyHow fast the model responds, critical in real-time applications like chatbots.
Multilingual supportWhether the model handles well the language the application needs.
Model size and complexityBigger models are usually more capable but also slower and more expensive.
CustomizationHow easy it is to adapt the model to a specific use case, whether through prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning.
Input/output lengthHow large a context window the model supports.

RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation), explained step by step

The problem RAG solves: a foundation model only "knows" what it learned during training, which has a cutoff date, and doesn't know your company's internal documents. Retraining the entire model every time your documents change would be very expensive and slow.

How RAG works, end to end: your documents are split into fragments (chunking) → each fragment is converted into an embedding (a numeric vector representing its meaning) → those embeddings are stored in a vector database → when a user asks a question, that question is also converted into an embedding → the system searches the vector database for the fragments with meaning closest to the question → those fragments are added as context to the prompt sent to the model → the model generates the answer using that retrieved context, instead of only its general knowledge.

The result: the model can answer with up-to-date, company-specific information, without having been retrained, and without changing a single internal weight of the model.
Vector database serviceWhat it is
Amazon OpenSearch ServiceSearch engine that also works as a vector database, widely used as the backend for Bedrock Knowledge Bases.
Amazon Aurora / RDS PostgreSQL with pgvectorExtension that adds the ability to store and search embeddings directly on top of an existing PostgreSQL relational database.

Agentic AI: autonomous agents and integration protocols

Agentic AI is the paradigm where the model doesn't just respond, it plans, decides which tool to use, and carries out multi-step tasks toward a goal, with memory of what happened. Amazon Bedrock AgentCore is AWS's managed platform for taking agents to production at scale: it works with any framework and is organized into independent modules such as Runtime (a serverless environment for running agents), Memory (short and long-term memory shareable across sessions), Gateway (turns APIs and Lambda functions into MCP-compatible tools), Identity (secure agent authentication), and Observability (tracing with OpenTelemetry).
ToolWhat it is
Strands AgentsOpen-source SDK built by AWS for building agents in Python and TypeScript in a few lines of code; works with multiple model providers and includes native MCP support.
KiroAWS's agentic IDE that applies "spec-driven development": the agent plans requirements, design, and tasks before generating code, instead of writing code straight from a loose instruction.
Model Context Protocol (MCP)Open protocol that standardizes how an agent connects to tools, data, and other MCP servers, avoiding a separate custom integration for every tool.
Context Engineering: the practice of designing systems that dynamically assemble the optimal set of information an LLM needs for a task, not just the prompt text. While prompt engineering is about what to ask, context engineering is about what to show the model: system instructions, user profile, conversation memory, tool or MCP definitions available, and context retrieved through RAG.

Ways to customize a model, from lowest to highest cost

TechniqueWhat it doesCost and speedWhen to use it
In-Context Learning / Prompting Guide the model by giving instructions or examples directly in the prompt, without modifying the model. The cheapest and fastest, immediate results. Simple tasks where explaining what's needed clearly is enough.
RAG Add relevant external context at query time, retrieved from a knowledge base. Cheap, requires preparing the vector database but doesn't retrain the model. You need the model to know specific or up-to-date information about your company.
Fine-Tuning Retrains and adjusts the model's internal weights using your own labeled data. More expensive and slower than RAG. You need the model to persistently change its underlying behavior, style, or domain, not just its access to specific information.
Model Distillation Transfers knowledge from a large teacher model to a smaller, cheaper student model, generating synthetic responses with the teacher to fine-tune the student. Cheaper and faster at inference than the teacher; distillation itself has a one-time extra cost. You need the quality of a large model but at lower cost and latency for a specific use case, without using your own labeled data the way fine-tuning does.
Pre-Training Train a foundation model completely from scratch, with massive amounts of data. The most expensive of all, requires infrastructure and data at massive scale. Almost never the right answer on the exam for an individual company; done by the large model providers.
Golden rule for the exam: if two options technically solve the problem, the one with lower cost and less maintenance effort usually wins, unless the question explicitly asks for the model's base behavior to change persistently, in which case the correct answer is fine-tuning.

Training and fine-tuning foundation models

ConceptWhat it is
Pre-TrainingMassive initial training of a foundation model from scratch, using general data from the internet and other sources.
Continuous Pre-TrainingContinuing to train an already pretrained model with additional data, generally from a specific domain, before moving to fine-tuning.

Preparing data for fine-tuning

Four factors determine the quality of a fine-tuning dataset: curation (cleaning and selecting the right data), governance (controlling who can use and modify that data), size (enough examples for the fine-tuning to be effective), and representativeness (the data should reflect the diversity of cases the model will face in production, to avoid bias).

Beyond technical metrics, the exam may ask about business metrics: whether the model increases team productivity, improves user engagement, or whether it's worth redesigning the whole task around the model's capabilities (task engineering) instead of just inserting it into an already existing process. The latest version of the exam guide adds specific business-alignment metrics: task completion rate, user satisfaction, and cost per interaction.

4. Guidelines for Responsible AI 14% of the exam
This domain tests whether you understand the ethical and practical implications of using AI, not just the technology. Most questions ask you to recognize a principle or a tool from a described situation.

Core principles of responsible AI

PrincipleQuestion it answersExample
Fairness"Does the model treat all groups fairly?"A credit approval model shouldn't systematically reject a demographic group for factors unrelated to actual risk.
Inclusivity"Does the system work well for the real diversity of users who will use it?"A speech recognition system trained and tested with different accents and languages, not just one.
Veracity and Robustness"Does the model stay reliable with unexpected inputs or inputs unlike its training data?"The model doesn't fail completely when faced with a question phrased in an unusual way.
Safety"Does the system avoid causing harm to the people who use it or are affected by it?"A medical chatbot that doesn't give advice that could put someone's health at risk.
Explainability"Can I understand and communicate why the model reached that decision?"Being able to explain to a customer why they were denied a loan, instead of just saying "the algorithm decided it."
Privacy and Security"Is the data used to train or query the model protected?"Not exposing users' personal information in the model's responses.
Controllability"Can I intervene, adjust, or stop the system if something goes wrong?"Having an emergency switch or a human review process before an automated decision executes.
Transparency"Do I clearly communicate to users what the system can and can't do?"Letting users know they're talking to an AI chatbot and not a person.
Governance"Are there organizational processes to oversee how AI is used?"An internal committee that reviews and approves new AI use cases before launching them to production.

Types of bias

It's worth distinguishing two sources of bias: data bias, already present in the training data before the model is even touched (for example, if 90% of the historical "good employee" examples belong to a single demographic group), and model or process bias, introduced by how the system was designed or trained, even if the original data was reasonably balanced.

Tools for detecting and monitoring

ToolWhat it doesPriority
Amazon SageMaker ClarifyDetects potential bias in training data and in the predictions of your own model, and helps explain which factors most influenced a given prediction.High
Amazon SageMaker Model MonitorWatches a model already in production to detect drift or quality violations against what's expected.Medium
Amazon Bedrock GuardrailsFilters input and output content on an already-consumed generative model (not your own), blocking unwanted topics or sensitive information.High
Amazon Augmented AI (A2I)Inserts human review into the automated decision flow when the model's confidence is low.Medium
SageMaker Clarify vs. Bedrock Guardrails, the key comparison in this domain: Clarify detects bias and explains predictions for a model you trained or are training yourself. Guardrails filters the input and output content of a generative model you're consuming, without access to its internal training data.

Transparency and explainability

ConceptWhat it is
Transparent modelIt's easy to understand how it reaches its decisions, like a simple decision tree.
Opaque model (black box)Its decisions are hard to interpret directly, as is often the case with large deep neural networks.
SageMaker Model CardsStructured documentation about the purpose, limitations, training data, and expected performance of your own model.
AWS AI Service CardsThe equivalent of Model Cards, but published by AWS for its own managed AI services, reporting their capabilities and limitations.
There's a frequent trade-off between interpretability and performance: simpler models tend to be easier to explain but less accurate, while more complex models tend to be more accurate but harder to interpret. Choosing one or the other depends on how critical it is to be able to explain each decision the system makes.

Additional considerations

TopicWhat to consider
Environmental impactTraining large models consumes a lot of energy; AWS promotes sustainability practices when selecting and using models.
Human-in-the-loopKeeping human oversight on high-impact decisions, instead of fully automating without review.
Legal risksIntellectual property infringement claims, biased model outputs, loss of customer trust, and end-user risks stemming from model hallucinations.
Characteristics of a good datasetInclusivity and diversity in the data, curated and verified sources, and datasets balanced across the relevant categories or groups.

Threats specific to AI systems

ThreatWhat it involvesPriority
Prompt Injection A user tries to manipulate the system instructions through the prompt itself so the model ignores its original restrictions. High
Data Poisoning Deliberately manipulating training data to bias or degrade a model's future behavior. Medium
Model Inversion / Extraction Trying to recover sensitive training information from the responses given by the already-trained model. Medium
The same general application security concepts apply here too: threat detection (identifying abnormal behavior around the AI system), vulnerability management (keeping the system's components up to date), and infrastructure protection (securing the network and compute resources that support the model).

6. Critical comparisons (memorize, no exceptions)
These are the comparisons that most often show up as distractors on the real exam, based on the patterns in your guides. If you know this table by heart, you'll solve a big share of the "trap" questions.
ComparisonPoint of distinction
RAG vs. Fine-tuningContext added at query time, without touching the model, vs. persistently retraining the model with your own data.
Bedrock vs. SageMakerConsuming and adapting already-trained foundation models, without managing infrastructure, vs. building and training your own machine learning models from the data.
Supervised vs. unsupervised learningData labeled with a known answer vs. patterns discovered in unlabeled data.
Precision vs. RecallOf what was flagged as positive, how much was correct, vs. of all the real positives, how many were detected.
Zero-shot vs. Few-shotNo examples in the prompt vs. guiding examples included in the prompt.
Overfitting vs. UnderfittingThe model memorizes the noise in the training data and fails on new data, vs. the model is too simple and fails even on the training data.
Comprehend vs. RekognitionText analysis vs. image and video analysis.
Transcribe vs. PollySpeech to text vs. text to speech.
Lex vs. KendraConversational chatbot vs. enterprise search over documents.
Personalize vs. ForecastPersonalized recommendations vs. time-series forecasting.
Foundation model vs. traditional ML modelPretrained, multitask, and general-purpose, vs. trained specifically for a single concrete task.
Data bias vs. model biasAlready present in the training data, vs. introduced by the system's design or training process.
SageMaker Clarify vs. Bedrock GuardrailsDetects bias and explains predictions for your own model, vs. filters input and output content on a consumed generative model.
Transparent model vs. opaque modelEasy to interpret how it reaches its decisions, vs. works like a black box that's hard to explain.
Real-time vs. batch inferenceImmediate response for an individual case, vs. massive processing of lots of data with no need for an instant response.
Fine-tuning vs. Model DistillationRetraining the model with your own labeled data, vs. transferring knowledge from a large teacher model to a smaller, cheaper student model.
Contextual grounding check vs. Automated Reasoning checksVerifies the response is backed by a source document and relevant to the question, vs. mathematically verifies the response complies with business rules encoded as formal logic.
LLM-as-a-Judge vs. human evaluationA model evaluates another model at scale and consistently, vs. people reviewing subjective quality like tone or usefulness.
